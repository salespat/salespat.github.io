The gap ALT 6,000 M
The talent was never in question. The playbook was never shared.

Walk the halls of any major semiconductor company and you will find them: engineers with doctorates, patents, and a decade of shipped silicon — who have never once been asked to lead.

The reason is not ability. The skills that earn a PhD are not the skills that earn a P&L. Executive communication, influence without authority, sponsorship, visibility — these are learned behaviors, taught informally, over coffee, to people who already look like the leadership team.

For first-generation immigrants raised to let the work speak for itself, that informal curriculum never arrives. Sagarmatha delivers it — deliberately, rigorously, and in a room where no one has to explain where they come from.

The climb ALT 7,500 M

Twelve weeks, four camps

Serious ascents move in stages. Each camp is earned before the next one opens.

Base CampWeeks 1–3

Know your terrain

Structured 360° feedback from peers and managers, a leadership diagnostic, and a private session to define your personal summit — the specific role, scope, and timeline you are climbing toward. Vague ambition doesn't survive Base Camp.

Camp IIWeeks 4–7

Practice under load

Weekly live workshops. You present your real projects to the cohort, run mock skip-levels and design reviews, and take direct feedback from people with no reason to flatter you. It is safe to be bad here so you can be excellent out there.

Camp IVWeeks 8–11

Lead in the wild

One visible leadership action at your actual company — lead a technical review, pitch an initiative, take over a stalled workstream — planned with your coach, executed by you, debriefed by the cohort. This is where the program leaves the classroom.

SummitWeek 12

Plant your flag

You present your leadership narrative to the full cohort and invited senior leaders, and leave with a twelve-month advancement roadmap. The summit is not the end of the climb. It's proof you can climb.

Questions ALT 8,600 M

Asked before every ascent

How much time does this actually take?

Plan for three hours per week: one two-hour live workshop and roughly an hour of preparation. The preparation is work you should be doing anyway — your promotion case, your executive narrative — so most participants describe it as redirected time, not added time.

Will my employer pay for it?

Frequently, yes. Most large technology companies hold learning and development budgets that cover external leadership programs, and a program with defined deliverables is an easy approval. We provide a one-page justification memo written for your manager, not for you.

Why a cohort instead of one-on-one coaching?

Because leadership is performed in front of people. A private coach can refine your thinking; only a room can pressure-test your presence. The cohort is also the product: eight peers who share your background, watch your growth, and remain your network long after week twelve.

I'm not Nepalese. Can I still apply?

The program is designed for the Nepalese diaspora, and most seats are reserved accordingly. A small number of seats in each cohort are open to engineers from other South Asian immigrant backgrounds who share the same path — deep technical credentials, thin leadership sponsorship. Tell us your story in the application.

What does it cost?

Founding-cohort pricing is disclosed during the application conversation and is set at half of what future cohorts will pay — in exchange for candid feedback that shapes the program. If the fee is the only thing standing between you and a seat, say so. We will find a way.
